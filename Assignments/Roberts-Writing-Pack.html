3. Literature Review

Maximum one page. Paragraphs only, no bullets. This draft runs about 450 words, which fits.

Rwanda has built one of the more complete health insurance systems in sub-Saharan Africa. Community based health insurance, administered nationally through the Rwanda Social Security Board, now reaches the large majority of the resident population, and successive health sector plans treat universal health coverage as settled policy rather than an ambition [1], [2]. For most Rwandan citizens the practical consequence is simple: treatment is sought with an insurance card, and the cost at the point of care is a defined share of the total. Pharmacy practice in the country reflects that arrangement. A prescription is issued, signed and stamped at a hospital or clinic and is tied to the patient's insurance cover, and the patient presents the prescription together with the insurance card at a pharmacy that accepts that insurer [3].

The system assumes something that does not hold for everyone living in Kigali. It assumes the patient already knows whether their insurer is accepted at a given pharmacy. Where cover is the national scheme, that assumption is safe, because acceptance is close to universal. Where cover is private, employer provided or held from another country, the assumption fails, and the failure is not recorded anywhere the person can reach. Pharmacies in Rwanda display the insurers they accept on a board or sticker at the entrance. That board is the only public record of acceptance, it is specific to that pharmacy, and it can be read only by a person standing in front of it. Our preliminary enquiries with pharmacy staff confirmed this arrangement and found no published directory that reproduces it.

Research on health access for migrants and mobile populations separates information barriers from financial ones, and reports that entitlement to care does not produce access when people cannot work out where their entitlement applies [4], [5]. The World Health Organization makes the same distinction in its universal health coverage material, treating usable information about available services as part of coverage rather than a separate concern [5]. Studies of international students and short stay residents in low and middle income countries describe the same experience: insurance held, provider unrecognised [7].

Existing tools address parts of this and stop short of the decision. Mapping applications locate pharmacies and report opening hours without recording which insurers each pharmacy accepts. Insurers publish provider lists, which are organised around formal networks rather than proximity and seldom extend to independent pharmacies. National health portals publish policy and service information at a level above the individual counter. The competitor scan in Section 12 documents this division. The information a newcomer needs therefore exists, but it is split between a physical sign, an insurer's network list and a general purpose map, and no single tool answers the question being asked: which pharmacy near me, open now, accepts my insurer.

Two things to check before this is final. The final sentence of the third paragraph and the whole of the fourth depend on David's competitor scan in Section 12. Read his section before you submit, and if his findings differ, correct ours rather than his. And [4] does double duty above for the migration evidence; if the source you find is about health access rather than migration volume, move its number to the sentence it actually supports.

1. Introduction and Mission Alignment

This section is graded but does not appear in the submission criteria list you were given. Do not let anyone delete it.

People arrive in Rwanda each year to study, to work, or to serve with an organisation, and every one of them has to learn a health system they did not grow up in. [[FILL §1: the number of international students or foreign residents in Rwanda, citing NISR [4]]] Most arrive insured, through an employer, a university arrangement, or a policy taken out before they left home. Insurance becomes useful only at the moment someone can find a provider that recognises it, and that moment rarely arrives at a convenient time. It arrives at night, or over a weekend, when a child has a fever or a wound needs dressing.

The difficulty is not medical and it is not financial. It is informational. A person who has lived in Kigali for years knows which pharmacy to walk into, and may not think of that as knowledge at all. A person who arrived last month does not. The gap between those two positions is what this project addresses. A case that a single pharmacy visit could resolve becomes an evening of telephone calls, borrowed advice and unnecessary travel, and it sometimes ends in a decision to wait and see. The costs fall on the newcomer first, in lost study or working hours and in money spent on journeys that finish at the wrong counter. They also fall on the people around them, because classmates, colleagues and neighbours absorb the search on behalf of whoever is unwell.

This work sits within a mission of improving health access for people who are new to a place, and it came from professional experience inside the team rather than from a gap in the literature. One of our members works with pharmacy systems in Rwanda and sees the same scene from the other side of the counter: a customer holding a card the pharmacy does not take, who had no way of knowing that before travelling. ALU asks students to work on challenges their own communities have. This is one, it is specific, and it is answerable with a mobile application.

2. Project Objectives

This is the only bulleted or numbered list allowed anywhere in the document. Every other section is paragraphs.

The objectives of this project are:

  1. To establish, through primary research with at least twelve participants across four groups, namely international students, other recently arrived residents, pharmacy staff, and insurance or hospital staff, how people currently identify a pharmacy that accepts their insurance, and what that process costs them in time, money and certainty.
  2. To document the pharmacy level insurance acceptance information that currently exists only on physical signage, and to test whether pharmacy staff would be willing to see it published.
  3. To design a mobile application that allows a user to select their insurer and see the pharmacies and hospitals near them that accept it, together with opening status and a route to the chosen destination.
  4. To produce an interactive high fidelity prototype of at least ten screens in Figma, covering the primary flow from onboarding through to reaching a pharmacy, and including an emergency pathway that works with the minimum information available.
  5. To evaluate the resulting proposal by mapping each significant design decision to the research finding that produced it.

4. Problem Statement and Hypothesis

Both are required, and they are two different things. Most teams submit only the first.

Problem statement. People who have recently arrived in Rwanda and who hold private, employer provided or foreign health insurance cannot reliably identify which nearby pharmacy will accept that insurance, and the information that would answer the question exists only on a physical board at each pharmacy entrance. The search is therefore conducted by trial. A person travels to pharmacies and reads the board on arrival, telephones an insurer during business hours, or relies on the recollection of somebody who arrived earlier. Each attempt costs time and travel, and the cost concentrates at the moment a person is least able to absorb it, which is when they, or someone in their care, is already unwell. In preliminary enquiries, pharmacy staff in Kigali confirmed that accepted insurers are displayed at the entrance, that cash is accepted at every pharmacy regardless of insurance, and that no directory of this information is published anywhere a newcomer can reach. The problem is not the absence of the information. It is the absence of any way to reach it before making the journey.

Hypothesis. We hypothesise that recently arrived residents holding non national health insurance spend more than [[FILL §4: the baseline average time, and the number of unsuccessful pharmacy journeys, reported by participants]] when identifying a pharmacy that accepts their cover, and that if insurance acceptance data were searchable by insurer and by proximity in a mobile application, then users would identify an accepting pharmacy within two minutes and without leaving their current location. We will test this by establishing the baseline cost from participant accounts in the interviews, and by measuring task completion time and the number of journeys required when participants use the prototype to find an accepting pharmacy for a stated insurer during usability testing.

Why this hypothesis is testable. It names a population (recently arrived residents holding non national insurance), a measured baseline (time and failed journeys, taken from the interviews), an intervention (acceptance data searchable by insurer and proximity), a predicted result (under two minutes, no journey), and a method of checking it (task timing in the prototype). If your interviews show the baseline is lower than you expect, change the number, not the structure.

15. Unique Value Proposition

For people who have recently arrived in Rwanda, [[App name]] shows which nearby pharmacies and hospitals accept their particular insurer, in seconds and before they leave the house, because it publishes in one searchable place the information that until now has existed only on the boards outside pharmacy doors.

The application reduces a search that currently involves travel, telephone calls and guesswork to a single query answered on the screen in the user's hand. The user selects the insurer named on their card, sees the pharmacies and hospitals near them that accept it, checks which are open, and leaves with a destination rather than a list of possibilities. The difference lies in the framing rather than in the data. Acceptance information already exists in public view, and mapping is widely available. Neither is assembled around the insurer, which is how the user's own question is put. A person does not ask where the pharmacies are. They ask where they can go with the card they are holding.

When you rewrite, strip these out

These are the constructions that make examiners suspect a generated draft. Academic writing does not need them.

  • Words to avoid: delve, landscape (abstract), underscore, testament, pivotal, crucial, robust, leverage, seamless, showcase, foster, intricate, multifaceted, realm, moreover and furthermore as paragraph openers.
  • Rule of three. If three items appear in a list, and one was added only to reach three, cut it.
  • "It is worth noting that", "It is important to note that". Delete the phrase and keep the sentence.
  • Negative parallelism. "It is not just about X, it is about Y" and "not only X but also Y". Write the point once.
  • Present participle clauses. "...highlighting the importance of access", "...reflecting a broader trend". Cut the clause, or turn it into a sentence with a subject.
  • Em dashes. Use a comma, a full stop, or brackets.
  • Bold and italics inside sentences. Reserved for section headings.
  • A rhetorical question answered in the next sentence. State the point.
  • A summary sentence that repeats the paragraph. Cut it.

The read aloud test. Read each paragraph out loud. Anywhere you would not speak that sentence to a person, rewrite it. That single test catches more than the whole list above.
