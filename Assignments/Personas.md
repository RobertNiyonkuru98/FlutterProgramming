## 1. Stephane Tchatchum

**Quote**
> *"in case of an emergency, I really don't know what I'm going to do."*

**One-line needs statement** (brief's format: *X is a Y who needs Z because ...*)
> Stephane is an infrequently-ill international student on private insurance who
> needs one place to check which nearby pharmacy is covered *and* stocked,
> because they currently reconcile the insurer's list against Google Maps by hand
> and have no plan at all for an emergency.

**Description**
A Cameroonian student who has been in Rwanda for one year. They are generally
healthy, so they interact with the health system rarely — and that is precisely
what leaves them exposed: they have never had to test their emergency plan, and they
haven't got one. For ordinary care they start from Britam's approved-provider
list, then cross-reference it against Google Maps to find the nearest covered
option, because the two pieces of information live in different places.
They have experienced the system from the waiting side too: they accompanied a sick
friend through Britam's verification process and watched it stall.

**Goals**
- Find the nearest covered pharmacy without cross-referencing two systems.
- Know which facility accepts their insurance **before** travelling.
- Have the emergency numbers and the nearest facility in one place.
- Be sure an in-network pharmacy actually has the drug in stock.

**Frustrations**
- The insurer's list and the map are separate; the reconciliation is manual.
- They do not know the emergency hotlines — "No, I don't think I know them."
- Slow insurance verification, felt most when their friend was unwell.
- A covered pharmacy can still be out of stock, wasting the trip.

## 2. Kami

**Quote**
> *"having an international health insurance does not necessarily mean that I can use it directly at local health facilities."*

**One-line needs statement**
> Kami is an international student holding cover that local clinics refuse who
> needs to know which facilities accept international insurance before setting
> out, because they currently pay upfront and claim reimbursement afterwards.

**Demographics**
| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Name | Kami |
| Age | `[ASK]` — not stated in the transcript |
| Origin | `[ASK]` — international student |
| Location | Rwanda |
| Relationship | `[ASK]` — not stated |
| Occupation | Student — ALU |
| Insurance | **International health insurance** — not accepted directly at most clinics |
| Interview | 27 Sep 2026, consent given on the recording |

**Description**
Kami holds international cover that turns out to be cover in theory only. At the
clinic counter the card is refused, so the pattern is always the same: pay out
of pocket, keep every receipt and medical document, then submit a claim and wait
to be reimbursed. The friction has shaped how care is chosen — quality matters,
but whether the clinic will accept the card matters more. Insurance has become
a phone call made first, before it becomes a claim made later.

**Goals**
- Find a facility that accepts international insurance before travelling.
- Avoid paying upfront for care that is covered.
- Understand what the cover actually pays for, and where.
- Get treated without first resolving payment.

**Frustrations**
- Clinics "do not recognize or accept my insurance".
- Unpredictable cost arriving exactly when care is urgent.
- Being their own claims department — receipts, documents, submit, wait.
- No published information on which facilities accept which plans.

**Description**
A salaried employee whose cover is a work benefit, and who treats it as one:
they moved from RSSB to Old Mutual and then to Britam because Britam offered more
per service "at a better price". They are a light user — roughly two years since
their last clinic visit — and their routine is settled: a small clinic first, a
hospital only if the clinic lacks the equipment. The mechanics of a visit are
what irritate them. They pay at the very end, and only then are they asked which
insurance they use; the prescription then sends them to a second location for the
medicine itself.

**Goals**
- Collect medicine without a second trip across town.
- Know the emergency number with confidence.
- Have medicine delivered, the way food is.
- Understand what the cover actually includes before they need it.

**Frustrations**
- The clinic and the pharmacy are two separate stops.
- They guess at the emergency number: *"it's either 912 or 112."*
- They assume but do not know the nearby pharmacy is open at night.
- They anticipate fraud and integrity problems in any delivery solution.

- Being asked for insurance *before* being treated.
- Framed as a moral failure: *"you might lose a life during that process."*
- Distance, not quality, is what decides where they end up.

## The four `[ASK]` fields

These are the only blanks. Age and relationship status are worth asking for
directly — one short message covers all four participants:

> Hi! Thanks again for the interview — it's really helping.
> Two quick details for the persona we're writing up: your **age**, and your
> **relationship status** (single / in a relationship — your choice)? Also,
> which **area of Kigali** do you stay in? Totally fine to skip anything you'd
> rather not share; we'll just note it as not stated.

If anyone declines, write **not stated** in the field. That is a legitimate
and defensible entry — an invented age is not.
