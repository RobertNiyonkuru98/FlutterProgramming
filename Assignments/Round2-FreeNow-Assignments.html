R1 — Source table (60 minutes — do this before writing a sentence)

Open a table. Fill it before you write prose.

# IEEE citation One statistic or finding you will use Page / quote URL Date accessed

Target: 5–7 sources. Minimum 5, and they must name Rwanda or East Africa. Where to look: RSSB (health insurance coverage and enrolment figures), Rwanda FDA, NISR (migration, urban population, household health spending), Ministry of Health / RBC, WHO, World Bank, and studies on health access for migrants and mobile populations. Only put a source in this table if you have actually opened it and read the relevant part. The brief verifies in-text citations against the reference list, and a mismatch scores zero for plagiarism. Do not pad.

R2 — Literature Review — 3 pts (90 minutes)

Maximum one page. Paragraphs only — no bullets, no numbered lists. Three movements:

  1. Context. Healthcare access for people who have recently arrived in a country: unfamiliar insurance, unfamiliar systems, language, and the emergency case.
  2. Evidence. The numbers from your table, each with an IEEE [n]. Everything cited, nothing asserted.
  3. The gap. What existing tools do not cover — and this is where our pharmacy-door insight lands: the acceptance information exists and is public, but it exists only physically, on a board at each pharmacy door, readable only by standing in front of that pharmacy.

Rules that will lose marks if ignored: number references [1]…[n] in order of first appearance · do not start three sentences in a row with "According to" · every sentence you write must be yours.

R3 — Introduction & Mission Alignment — 3 pts (45 minutes)

This section is not in the submission-criteria list you were given, but it is graded. Say so to the team so nobody deletes it.

One to two paragraphs: the community challenge we are addressing, why it matters, and credible data on the social and economic significance — cited, from R1. Write it academically: cohesive and analytical, not promotional.

R4 — Problem Statement + Hypothesis — 3 pts (45 minutes)

Both are required, and they are two different things. Two short paragraphs in the document.

Paragraph 1 — the problem, user-centred and grounded in preliminary research. The version we already have is the right shape; it now needs citations from R1 and the wording tightened.

Paragraph 2 — a testable hypothesis. This is the part people lose marks on. A testable hypothesis names a population, a measurable outcome and a comparison — not a wish. Use this frame:

We hypothesise that [population] currently [measurable current cost — time, trips, money], and that if [our intervention] then [measurable improvement], which we will test by [how — the baseline reported in interviews, plus task testing in the prototype].

The current-cost number will come from the interviews, so write it as [[FILL FROM INTERVIEWS — David]] and keep going. The measurable improvement must be something a prototype can actually demonstrate (time to find an accepting pharmacy, number of failed trips, whether they can identify the nearest open one).

R5 — UVP + Reframed Problem — 5 pts (60 minutes)

Two separate deliverables in this one row. Most teams submit only the first and lose half the marks.

(a) The UVP — one single, clear, compelling statement. It must contain the key benefit, how it solves the need, and what makes it different. Yours is strong because the difference is concrete — the existing thing is a board on a door, and nobody has put it online. Write it as one or two sentences a stranger understands on first read.

(b) The reframed problem — from the USER'S perspective. First person, in their voice, supported by what they told us. Not a repeat of R4. Something in this register:

"I have insurance I can't use, because I can't find out who takes it without walking there."

Leave a marked slot for a verbatim interview quote to sit beside it — that quote is what makes this row exemplary.
