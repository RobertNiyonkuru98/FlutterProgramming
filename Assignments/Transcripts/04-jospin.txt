Interview 04 - participant "Jospin" (the interviewer addresses him as "Joel" in the transcript)
Interviewer: David

Interviewer: Yes, Joel, can you hear me?
Interviewee: Yes, I can hear you, Mr. David.
Interviewer: Yeah, thank you so much for allowing us to have an interview with you. I want to start by introducing ourselves. Actually, we are conducting our research. So, we are from African Leadership University, and we are conducting our research basically on accessing healthcare, pharmacy, and insurance here in Rwanda. So, before we start, I want to just make sure that you agree that this interview will be recorded for academic research purposes.
Interviewee: Yes, I'm glad. It's fine with me. You can record it.
Interviewer: Yeah, thank you so much. So, the first question will be: what is the type of health insurance you currently use here in Rwanda?
Interviewee: So currently, I'm using *Mutuelle*. Yeah, that's the one that I'm using now.
Interviewer: Okay, nice, very nice. Can you walk me through the last time you needed to visit a clinic, a hospital, or a pharmacy?
Interviewee: Yeah, so it's not a while ago. It's not too long since I've been to the hospital. This time, I think it was two months ago. I had to visit the hospital for a quick full-body medical checkup. It was not just because I was sick; I just wanted to check up on my body. I went to the hospital—I think it's called Kibagabaga Hospital. Yeah, I think that's the last time I went to the hospital.
Interviewer: Okay, nice, nice. So, let's say, for example, you feel unwell. How do you usually decide where to go or what clinic or hospital to visit first?
Interviewee: So I would actually say that it's more of the distance—where the hospital is located—than what kind of hospital it is. So I usually see the distance between my home and the hospital. That makes it easier to decide where I should go. So, it's basically the distance from my house to the hospital.
Interviewer: Nice, nice, nice. Another question would be, let me say, for example, you bumped into an accident and you need an emergency service, and you wanted to call an ambulance or a hospital. What is the number you can use to call an ambulance?
Interviewee: I've not used an ambulance before, but for a fact, I know it's 112—the ambulance number for Rwanda.
Interviewer: Nice, nice, that's true. Have you ever needed medicine late at night or outside of regular hours? Can you tell me more about the experience of finding those medicines at night?
Interviewee: Uh, I've had a bad experience with that. Last time, I had flu—and you know, the flu of these days is more harsh than flu back in the days. So I had this flu; it was more like malaria. I needed a quick checkup or some quick relief pills, but it was—what time was it? It was not midnight, but a few hours away from midnight. It was more and more difficult to find an open pharmacy, and the hospital or clinic near me was also closed. The only place I could go was to a hospital, and the hospitals are far away from my house. It was a bit challenging and a horrible experience.
Interviewer: I understand, I understand. Is there anything else about accessing healthcare in Rwanda that you think we should know, or is there anything else you would like to change about healthcare in Rwanda? Could you let us know about it?
Interviewee: Yeah, I think because we do have a system—the system of *Mutuelle*—and the government has established it, thankfully, we give great thanks to the government for that, and as you know, we do pay for it, right? The one thing that I would like to be changed is how people go to the hospitals: instead of being treated first, they are asked for insurance. Because you can check them up quickly and give them medicine or give them the help they need while looking for if they already paid for the insurance. Because you might lose a life during that process. I think it should be easier to provide healthcare and health services first, rather than asking for insurance.
Interviewer: Nice, nice. So yeah, I appreciate your time, and that brings us to the end of this interview. Truly appreciate you being here with us.
Interviewee: Thank you, Mr. David, and I hope this was helpful.
Interviewer: Yeah, sure. Thank you so much. Wish you all the best.
Interviewee: Yeah, thank you.
