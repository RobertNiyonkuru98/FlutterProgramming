Interview 02 - participant "Kami"
Interviewer: David
Note: the interviewer's own transcription renders the name "Cam" in places. The
participant's name is Kami.

Interviewer: Thank you, Cam, for allowing us to have this interview with you.
Kami: Thank you, too.
Interviewer: Yeah. So I want to just start with introducing us. So basically, we are doing research—we are from African Leadership University—and we want to understand our students' actual real-life experience on healthcare, pharmacy, and emergency services here in Rwanda. So before we begin, I want to just make sure that you agree that this interview will be recorded for academic research purposes. I hope that's okay with you.
Kami: Yes, it's okay with me.
Interviewer: Okay, nice one, thank you. So, the first question will be: what is the type of health insurance that you currently use here in Rwanda?
Kami: So I have an international health insurance. However, one of the challenges is that many health clinics and hospitals in Rwanda do not accept my insurance directly. So I sometimes have to pay for the treatment myself and then try to get reimbursed by my insurance company.
Interviewer: Oh, that makes sense. That makes sense. Can you walk me through the last time you needed to visit a clinic or a hospital here in Rwanda?
Kami: I would say the last time I needed healthcare, I went to a nearby clinic first. When I presented my international insurance, they told me that they did not accept it. I had to pay for the consultation and any medication myself. Afterwards, I kept my receipts and medical documents so that I could submit a claim to my insurance provider for reimbursement.
Interviewer: Okay, nice, nice, nice. Can you tell me, when you feel unwell—maybe you are having malaria—how do you usually decide where to go or what clinic or hospital to visit first?
Kami: So I usually look for a nearby clinic or hospital that I know has good services, but I also consider whether they accept my international insurance. Sometimes I contact my insurance company first to find out which healthcare providers they work with. This can be inconvenient, especially when I need care urgently.
Interviewer: Okay, okay, nice, nice. Let's say, for example, you have an emergency. Maybe you bumped into someone who just had an accident. What contact number would you call in that scenario?
Kami: In an emergency, I would call 112 for an ambulance. I would also contact my insurance provider's emergency assistance number if necessary, particularly if I need to arrange or confirm coverage.
Interviewer: Okay, sounds good. Have you ever needed medicines late at night or outside regular hours? Could you take me through the experience you went through?
Kami: Yes, when I have needed medicine late at night, finding an open pharmacy can be challenging. The situation is also more complicated because I cannot always use my insurance directly. I may have to pay out of pocket and then submit the receipt to my insurance company for reimbursement.
Interviewer: Okay. Is there anything you would like to address about the healthcare here in Rwanda?
Kami: Maybe one of the biggest challenges for me is that having an international health insurance does not necessarily mean that I can use it directly at local health facilities. Many clinics do not recognize or accept my insurance, so I have to pay upfront and request a reimbursement later. This can be stressful and expensive, especially for unexpected or emergency healthcare. It would be helpful if more healthcare providers had direct agreements with international insurance companies, and if there were clear information about which facilities accept different insurance plans.
Interviewer: Okay, nice one, nice one. Yeah, I think that brings us to the end of our interview. We want to appreciate your time, Kami, for taking your time to talk to us. We truly appreciate it.
Kami: Thank you, too.
Interviewer: Yeah, thank you, thank you.
