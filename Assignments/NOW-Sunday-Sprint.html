2. The honest-numbers rule — read before you write §5

We report the number of interviews we actually did, and we report it against the evidence folder. Nothing else is acceptable.

The interview row is graded on evidence: a script, a consent record, audio, and notes, per participant. If §5 says twelve while the Drive holds five folders, that discrepancy is the single most damaging thing in the document, and it is trivially checkable. A team that says "we completed six in-depth interviews and report them fully" scores better than one that claims twelve and cannot produce them.

So: never inflate the count. Make depth the argument instead. Six participants with verbatim quotes, sensory notes and a filled Claims → Evidence table reads as rigorous. Twelve names with no audio reads as invented, because it is.

4. The 15-minute script

Fifteen minutes is deliberate. It is long enough for an empathy map and short enough that people say yes and that you can run several. Ask these in order, and ask them the same way every time.

# Question
1 Screener: When did you arrive in Rwanda, and which health insurance do you hold?
2 Walk me through the last time you needed medicine here. What happened, step by step?
3 How did you decide which pharmacy to go to?
4 How did you find out whether they accept your insurance?
5 What did you do when you weren't sure?
6 How long did that take, and how many trips did it cost you? (get a real number, not "a while")
7 What if it were late at night, or a weekend? What would you do then?
8 What would have made that easier?

Three rules, and they decide whether the data is usable:

  • No yes/no questions. "Do you use X?" ✗ → "How do you handle X?" ✓
  • Never describe our app before asking about the problem. The moment you do, every answer after it is contaminated and the interview is worthless for the empathy map.
  • Write quotes verbatim, in their words. Two or three per person. Those exact sentences are what fill the empathy maps, the personas and the Claims → Evidence table. Paraphrase and you lose the marks.

5. The pharmacy walk-in — do it this afternoon

One hour, two people, two or three pharmacies. It is the highest-value single action left today, for four reasons:

  1. It is the only way to get the pharmacy and pharmacy-staff segment, which is what the rubric's diversity row is measuring.
  2. It produces the best sensory notes — the sign on the door, the queue, the smell of the dispensary. Those four labels (Sights, Sounds, Feels, Smells) are the most-missed item on this assignment, and they cannot be written from a phone call.
  3. It gets you two interviews in one hour, for free.
  4. You will photograph the board. That photo is direct evidence for our entire problem statement, and it belongs in the document.

Be honest and be brief. Ask for the manager or the pharmacist, say you are students, and ask five questions: which insurers do you accept · how do you display that · do people ask you this often · do you get customers holding cards you don't take · would you mind being listed in an app.

And if a pharmacy is shut when you get there, that is not a wasted trip. It is a finding: "at 15:00 on a Sunday, the pharmacy on X road was closed" is evidence for exactly the emergency problem we are claiming exists. Record the time, the location and a photo. Write it in §5.
